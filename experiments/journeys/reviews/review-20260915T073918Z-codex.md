J10 establishes a 43.75% difference between the best observed full-prompt and 8:1 replay throughput on the H100, under the particular workload, configuration, and 90-second measurement windows used. It does not establish twice the sustainable production capacity, a recurrent-state residency ceiling, or lower cost per successful coding task. The claimed 100-sequence hardware ceiling is confounded by the load generator’s default 100-connection client limit. The throughput arithmetic largely reproduces, but the run count, dispersion claims, price basis, and characterization of the 16:1 gain need correction. Quality was not measured at the benchmark operating points, and essential artifacts needed to verify checkpoint identity and independently rescore quality are absent. The central business claim is currently supportable only as a hypothesis for further testing, not as a capacity or fleet-budget commitment.

## Dimension 1: serving benchmark design

### F-1-1: The 90-second measurements do not establish steady-state throughput. Severity: blocking. Confidence: high.

`experiments/bench/loadgen.py` starts all closed-loop workers together, always starts at corpus entry zero, waits only 15 seconds, and stops issuing replacements at 105 seconds. A worker immediately replaces each completed request. This is a valid instrument for a finite-window closed-loop replay; it is not sufficient by itself to establish sustained capacity.

The raw timing shows substantial startup structure:

| Run | Completions in 15–45 s | 45–75 s | 75–105 s |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| R`B1_full_c32_r1.json` | 0 | 32 | 32 |
| R`B1_gist8_c32_r1.json` | 25 | 35 | 32 |
| R`B4nocache_full_c16_r1.json` | 0 | 0 | 9 |

The first full c=32 completion occurs at **45.60 seconds**. The cache-off full c=16 run does not complete its first request until **77.33 seconds**.

Consequently, the large cache-off ratio at c=16 includes a full arm that spends most of the nominal measurement window completing its initial batch. It may reflect a real performance difference, but it is not a demonstrated long-run ratio.

Longer runs spanning multiple complete corpus traversals, with stable completion-rate and queue traces before measurement begins, would change this assessment. The fixed-output limitation is disclosed; the absence of demonstrated steady state is not.

### F-1-2: The open-loop experiment does not resolve a sustainable capacity boundary. Severity: blocking. Confidence: high.

`open_loop()` generates exponentially distributed intervals, but every invocation uses the same default seed, zero. Both repeats therefore reproduce essentially the same arrival realization and the same corpus prefix.

At the claimed limits:

- Full, target 18 rpm: **22 measured requests per repeat**, p95 **8.3953 and 8.3981 seconds**.
- Gist, target 36 rpm: **45 measured requests per repeat**, p95 **7.6983 and 7.6714 seconds**.

Full at target 27 rpm has mean p95 **8.6507 seconds**, only **0.1218 seconds** above its **8.5289-second** threshold. No uncertainty interval establishes that this point genuinely violates a population p95 requirement.

The arm-specific “twice unloaded median” objective is a defensible normalized slowdown criterion. It is applied consistently and is stricter in absolute seconds for gist. It is not a customer-facing common latency commitment. With a common **9-second** threshold, the same observations select **27 versus 36 target rpm**, producing **1.33×**, not 2×.

The paper’s statement that open-loop measurement avoids quantization is also false: the arrival ladder is discrete. Even assuming monotonic, stable latency, the observations locate boundaries between tested rates rather than identify exact capacities.

Use independent arrival seeds, substantially longer runs, a prespecified common service objective, uncertainty around the tail, and finer rates around the boundary. Record arrival and completion rates and queue growth separately.

### F-1-3: Client latency, engine counters, and throughput use different measurement populations. Severity: major. Confidence: high.

`summarize()` selects requests by **start time**, then includes their complete latency even when much of it occurs after issuance stops. Engine counters are collected before warm-up and after all requests drain; metric maxima and means likewise cover warm-up, measurement, and drain.

For R`B1_gist8_c32_r1.json`:

- **92** requests enter the measured latency cohort.
- **32** of those finish after 105 seconds.
- Drain ends at approximately **132.06 seconds**.
- Engine generation totals include **124 × 200 = 24,800 tokens**, whereas the reported measurement-window output numerator is **92 × 200 = 18,400**.

For full c=32, half the measured cohort finishes after issuance stops. Its latency distribution therefore partly describes a draining system, not a system maintained indefinitely at the stated load.

This does **not** justify mechanically dividing the published numerator by the drain duration: that would create a different, also mismatched estimator. Closed-loop replacement explains why the archived start and completion counts agree.

The content-bearing TTFT change is present. However, `METRIC_KEYS` contains no engine TTFT metric, and the repository contains no demonstrated TTFT cross-check matching the paper’s claim. Synchronous `/metrics` requests also block the client event loop; their effect was not instrumented.

## Dimension 2: fairness of the two arms

### F-2-1: Gist-only tuning does not establish a fairly optimized full-prompt baseline. Severity: blocking. Confidence: high.

The tuning score in `bench_chain.sh` is simply throughput if errors are zero. No p95 constraint is evaluated, and no candidate is measured on full prompts.

The two best candidates tie at **68 rpm**; strict `>` selection retains the first, with 256 configured sequences. There is no evidence that this configuration maximizes full-arm throughput, common-SLO goodput, or a joint objective.

The matched c=8 comparison is particularly revealing:

- Original configuration: full **42.6667**, gist **48.0**.
- Selected configuration: full **34.6667**, gist **48.0**.

Thus the larger c=8 ratio comes entirely from a slower full baseline in that comparison. The +44% headline additionally compares different selected peak concurrencies.

This does not prove that fair tuning would remove the gain. It proves that the explanation “same flags, therefore the gap is the prompt” is insufficient. Measure every candidate on both arms and report both a common-configuration comparison and independently optimized arm performance under the same service requirement.

### F-2-2: Cache history and the evaluated request mix are not held fixed. Severity: major. Confidence: high.

Beyond prompt length, the arms differ in the following ways:

| Difference | Evidence and likely consequence |
|---|---|
| Prefix-cache contents and history | One server persists across B1–B3. `prewarm()` issues requests for 25 seconds; it does not reset the cache or guarantee equal corpus coverage. Previous arms and lower concurrencies affect subsequent cache state. Direction is unresolved. |
| Initial arm position | Full starts first in two of three H100 blocks. B3/B4 always run full first; 16:1 always runs gist first. This leaves time/order effects correlated with arm. |
| Requests reached within a run | Each run restarts at corpus entry zero. Faster arms consume more entries. At c=32, each full run issues **96** total requests and each gist run **124**. They do not measure the same complete 198-request workload. |
| Requests excluded as warm-up | Filtering by issuance time removes different corpus prefixes at different operating points. Prompt-length and session composition therefore vary with arm and concurrency. |
| Generated content | Both arms generate 200 tokens, but outputs can differ. Their timing result does not establish equivalent useful work. |
| Session semantics | The generator ignores session identity after loading. It can issue several recorded turns from one session simultaneously, using prerecorded history rather than the current generated response. “Concurrency” counts request workers, not live developers or agent sessions. |

The full and gist8 arms do share the same served checkpoint within a block, according to the orchestrator. That avoids an arm-specific model-weight change. It does not remove the other differences.

The prompt-length sequence reconstructed from the first 198 arrivals at c=256 shows exactly **15,171 fewer input tokens per gist8 request**. This verifies a length correspondence; it cannot verify token content or exact semantic pairing without the corpus.

### F-2-3: The 16:1 comparison changes more than compression ratio. Severity: major. Confidence: high.

`bench_provision.sh` ships corrected `r8v2` and earlier `r16` bundles. `STATE` records **2,171** rows for r8v2 and **1,099** for r16. These are different training histories and span definitions, not a controlled ratio-only ablation.

The reconstructed full and full16 prompt-length sequences match across the first 198 requests. But gist8 removes **15,171** tokens per request, while gist16 removes **16,436 or 16,437**, consistent with more than simply halving the corrected gist rows.

The 16:1 checkpoint also has its own server launch and fixed gist-first order. Its own full control is necessary and present, but does not control away differences between the 8:1 and 16:1 training recipes.

The supported conclusion is that this particular earlier r16 checkpoint showed **26.5625%** peak uplift over its control, versus **43.75%** for r8v2. It does not establish a general serving optimum at 8:1.

## Dimension 3: statistical treatment

### F-3-1: The reported dispersion is both misstated and narrower than the relevant uncertainty. Severity: major. Confidence: high.

`analyze.py` computes `statistics.pstdev`, a descriptive population SD over the observed repeats. It neither calculates the registered confidence intervals nor estimates uncertainty across workloads, hosts, or arrival realizations.

Examples from raw request counts:

- H100 full c=1 and c=4: population SD **0.6285 rpm**, sample SD **0.7698**.
- H100 gist8 c=128: population SD **3.9252**, sample SD **4.8074**.
- Displayed gist16 c=64: population SD **1.3333**, sample SD **1.8856**.
- H200 full c=64: counts **89 and 128**, rates **59.3333 and 85.3333**, population SD **13.0 rpm**.

The CTO note’s “under 1 percent” claim is false even at H100 full c=1: `0.6285/13.1111 = 4.79%`. The paper’s ≤0.6 caption also fails for its displayed 16:1 series.

Repeated deterministic requests on one persistent server measure repeatability conditional on that setup. They are not independent replicates of the deployment population. Zero count dispersion at many points does not eliminate systematic error or workload uncertainty.

### F-3-2: Request-count resolution and short-run phase make the reported peak location insufficiently resolved. Severity: major. Confidence: high.

A single completion changes a 90-second rate by **0.6667 rpm**. Near-synchronous cohorts can change counts by much more than one request when a window boundary moves.

The full arm records exactly **64** measured requests at both c=16 and c=32, in every repeat. The aggregator calls c=16 the peak because it encounters the tied point first, not because c=16 is statistically superior.

Gist c=16 and c=32 differ by only **three requests per repeat**: **89 versus 92**, or **2 rpm**. Given their different startup phases, correlated corpus progression, and short windows, the data do not establish that c=32 is the long-run optimum.

Taking the maximum of a concurrency sweep is defensible for exploratory reporting. Calling it a validated peak requires a separate confirmation run, preferably using a fresh workload order and longer windows. The distinction is absent from the executive claims.

## Dimension 4: mechanism

### F-4-1: A client connection limit invalidates the claimed measurement of a 100-sequence hardware ceiling. Severity: blocking. Confidence: high.

Both load modes create `aiohttp.ClientSession()` without configuring its connector. Aiohttp’s default connector allows **100 simultaneous connections**, and requests wait for a connection when that limit is exhausted. See the primary [aiohttp connector documentation](https://docs.aiohttp.org/en/stable/client_reference.html#connectors).

The archived B2 metric samples show:

| Card | Arm | Maximum running | Maximum running + waiting |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| H100 | Full | 54 | 100 |
| H100 | Gist | 66 | 100 |
| H200 | Full | 100 | 100 |
| H200 | Gist | 100 | 100 |

This is directly consistent with the client admitting no more than 100 simultaneous requests to the server. At c=256, additional workers can wait in the client connection pool, and their wait is included in client TTFT and E2E.

**Inference:** the exact H200 plateau is likely client-imposed. Even without establishing that inference conclusively, the experiment cannot distinguish it from a recurrent-cache ceiling.

The +44% peak comparison at c≤32 is not directly affected by this particular limit. Claims about c=128/256 saturation, residency, and the hardware mechanism are affected. Rerun with an explicitly enlarged connector and record client connection wait, server admission, scheduler wait, and recurrent-cache allocation.

### F-4-2: Equal sampled residency does not identify the source of the throughput difference. Severity: major. Confidence: high.

Even absent the client defect, equal maximum running counts plus unequal throughput would establish neither equal average occupancy nor the cause of different service times.

Possible contributors include prefill cost, decode attention cost, batching, cache hits, scheduling, and preemption. The sampled maximum of `num_requests_running` does not partition these effects.

H100 B2 additionally records **18 full versus 41 gist preemptions** in the sampled counter deltas. The paper’s simple turnover explanation omits that complication. H200’s **1.71×** B2 rate ratio is a short-run observation, not a causal decomposition.

The existing aggregate engine sums cover unequal request populations and entire run lifetimes. They do not substantiate the paper’s “about 60 percent less prefill work” as a measured mechanism; approximately that fraction describes input reduction.

A discriminating experiment must measure actual uncached prefill tokens, per-request prefill/decode/queue times, average active occupancy, and separate attention/recurrent allocation while varying prompt length at a fixed admitted load.

## Dimension 6: training and quality

### F-6-1: Quality preservation was not tested at any J10 serving operating point. Severity: blocking. Confidence: high.

J10 calls `/v1/completions` directly with token IDs, `temperature=0`, `ignore_eos=true`, and exactly 200 output tokens. It does not run the agent, execute tools, retain generated text for quality scoring, or count successful tasks.

The corrected r8v2 checkpoint has a stored J5 hard-suite score of **16/16**, or **15/15 after excluding the defective delete probe**. That was a different evaluation environment and serving configuration. J10 rebuilds the checkpoint from private artifacts; matching row counts and aggregate row-change statistics do not cryptographically establish that it is the exact evaluated object.

The paper discloses task reuse, single runs, and missing generalization. Those are limitations already acknowledged. The additional defect is combining historical development scores with J10 performance to imply quality-constrained deployment savings.

An independently scored live-agent workload must run on the exact hashed benchmark checkpoint, under the selected serving configuration and offered load.

### F-6-2: The quality instruments cannot support the broader parity language used in the summaries. Severity: major. Confidence: high.

`eval_hard.py` and `eval_checks.py` include checks for file existence, function names, document length, references, and model-written tests. Some checks are meaningful task probes; collectively they do not establish behavioral equivalence.

Examples:

- Hard task 7 passes when a file remains present, although the task asks for deletion.
- Hard task 8 accepts `NOT_PERMITTED`.
- Several code checks rely partly on tests produced by the agent being evaluated.
- `session_calls()` drops non-200 records rather than making infrastructure failures invalidate the task.
- `eval_coverage.py` credits required tool-name occurrence even if execution fails.

The corrected r8v2 score includes a task with **five unswapped turns**. The paper explains that child-agent sessions can use the full prompt. That is a disclosed hybrid condition, not an all-gist quality result.

The stored earlier r16 hard score is **12/16**, not parity across all exams. “Equal task scores on our suites, every ratio” in the deck is false. Restrict parity language to the named suite, checkpoint, checker, and single development run.

### F-6-3: The distillation objective is identifiable, but its target fidelity and provenance remain unverified. Severity: major. Confidence: high.

`train.py` implements KL from the renormalized cached top-K teacher distribution to the student’s full-vocabulary distribution. Its batch and per-response reductions implement the stated formulas.

The remaining problems are those deferred after the first review:

- Teacher caches are joined to datasets by integer index, without sequence, tokenizer, checkpoint, or response hashes.
- `teacher_cache.py` labels actual-token log-probability availability as membership inside top-K; these are different conditions.
- `dataset.py` reconstructs responses from parsed blocks and does not establish emitted-token identity.
- There is no supplied HF/vLLM prompt-and-logit parity test.
- Retained top-32 mass does not verify omitted-tail KL or downstream equivalence.
- No matched trained-versus-untrained gist control establishes how much observed task performance is attributable to training.

The paper now states several of these limitations correctly. They remain unresolved scientific dependencies, not closed review findings.

## Dimension 10: next experiments

### F-10-1: The next work must test the disputed causal and economic links before extending the ratio sweep. Severity: blocking. Confidence: high.

Ranked by expected information per GPU-hour:

1. **Remove and test the client ceiling.** On the H200, compare explicit connector limits of 100 and at least 512, full versus r8v2, at c=128 and c=256. Prewarm the required shapes, then maintain load for several minutes. Record connection wait, server running/waiting counts, recurrent allocation, and KV usage. Roughly an hour can determine whether the central residency observation was client-imposed. If residency exceeds 100, withdraw the original mechanism immediately.

2. **Confirm or kill the throughput headline under controlled replay.** On the H100, test both the original and selected configurations on both arms at c=8,16,32. Use paired, hashed corpus schedules; explicit cache reset followed by identical prewarming; randomized block order; and independent workload offsets. Warm to stable rates, then measure at least several complete corpus traversals. Confirm the selected configuration and peak on a separate block. Follow with longer open-loop tests around the boundary using independent seeds and one common absolute SLO. Stop expanding the sweep if the +44% or 2× claims disappear under these controls.

3. **Measure useful work on the exact served checkpoint.** Freeze untouched tasks, repositories, fixtures, checkers, and a paired noninferiority margin before evaluation. Compare full prompt, corrected trained gist, and an untrained mean-initialized gist control; use independent expected outputs rather than relying on agent-written tests. Include dynamic values, rare tools, and explicit child-agent accounting. Begin with a small failure-finding stage, then expand until the prespecified quality comparison is decisive. Run the full-versus-trained comparison both unloaded and at the proposed production load. Count successful completed tasks per GPU-hour, including retries and fallback work. Material quality regression kills the deployment claim even if token replay remains faster.

4. **Test the economic substitution only after the preceding gates pass.** Give each card and arm equivalent tuning effort, then replay the validated live workload at the same latency and quality requirement. Compare successful-task goodput using both quoted and effective rental prices. A cache-enabled trimmed or lazily loaded tool baseline belongs here. If cheaper-card substitution depends on one favorable spot quote, unmatched SLOs, or weaker quality, retain it as a local pricing observation rather than a fleet claim.

Further compression-ratio training has lower immediate information value than these experiments.
